\textbf{Summer Funding Plan}\par

\begin{indented}
I will use the Hamilton Award to enable full-time commitment to research this summer, allowing me to advance the goals of my project more quickly. \par 


Tissues and individual cells are poroelastic, consisting of a background matrix suffused by a solvent carrying solutes of diverse sizes. In vivo, tissues undergo dynamic stresses, causing deformations which induce advective flows. Size-dependent transport of proteins and vesicles ensues; solutes larger than the pore size of the tissue are greatly hindered by interactions with the mesh, while smaller solutes move more freely. Experiments examining the macroscale dynamics of poroelastic media demonstrate enhanced concentration of large solutes in cyclically strained hydrogels, but there remains a dearth of studies probing the intricate interactions between solutes, pore size, stress gradients, and microfluidics during dynamic strains in living tissue and cells, and thus the roots of these emergent behaviors remain unclear.\par

My work to date has consisted of tracking single nanoparticles in strained poroelastic media, providing the first live insight into the nanoscopic dynamics of these systems. I have shown that both static and dynamic strains increase long-term localization and decrease transport compared to unstrained controls, while dynamic strain induces asymmetric advective transport of large solutes during compression and relaxation, creating a pumping effect. My work to date has been completed with the goal of applying identical methodologies to live-cell imaging in order to understand how solute distribution in the nucleus, a heterogeneous poroelastic environment often subjected to dynamic strains, drives gene transcription.\par

This summer, I will extend the study to living systems by tracking transcription factors (TFs) in live cells to understand how strains impact their transport and distribution, ultimately altering gene expression. By connecting imposed dynamic strains to the redistribution of TF I will uncover a novel mechanism for how cells can respond to external stress. Doing so will require single particle tracking of fluorescently labelled TFs in live cellular nuclei subject to controlled strains.\par

To accomplish this, an existing cell line will be treated with non-toxic HALO-tag solution to fluorescently label transcription factors. The cells will be imaged on a light sheet fluorescence microscopy system of my own design operating with low phototoxicity within a climate-controlled incubation system, allowing for extended experiments on healthy cells. Precise strain will be applied during live imaging using a 20$\mu m$ spherical indenter mounted on a piezoelectric-actuated atomic force microscope cantilever. The strain system is mounted on a micro-manipulation stage, allowing for precise positioning.
Fluorescently-labelled TFs will be imaged using highly inclined laminated optical sheet fluorescence microscopy (HILO) to acquire high-framerate time series of the particles. Individual TFs will be tracked using TrackMate, an open-source state-of-the-art tracking platform, and the resulting tracks will be analyzed via msdanalyzer (an open-source MATLAB plugin for single particle tracking) and personal MATLAB scripts. As an extension, a unique microscope enabling enables simultaneous atomic force microscopy and HILO will be used to produce simultaneous particle tracking and microrheological data.\par 
I have applied this approach to successfully track comparable nanoparticles in strained poroelastic hydrogels at up to 800 frames per second; furthermore, recent collaborations with Dr. Kerry Bloom of UNC Biology have demonstrated single particle tracking of HALO-tagged proteins in the nucleus. Investing three years of my work has lead to the development of a system with capabilities necessary for this undertaking; along with my recent experiments in hydrogels, this positions me to uncover new phenomena in living cells this summer.
\end{indented}
