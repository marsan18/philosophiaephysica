Dr. Pratap, \par 
I write regarding the exciting opportunity to work a temporary instructor of Physics I and II at UNC-Greensboro, as advertised via announcement by Dr. Pratap. I am currently a doctoral candidate at UNC-Chapel Hill; I have completed all requirements for my degree but my dissertation, and anticipate defending my thesis in Spring 2027. I have defended a thesis to earn my Master's of Science in Physics, and while I remain a full-time graduate student at UNC, as a Royster Fellow with non-service funding I am available to fulfill the position this spring. My extensive experience as a physics instructor includes a long history of working with students enrolled in the aforementioned courses; I have acted as an assistant instructor at both Wake Forest and UNC-Chapel Hill in various roles across their respective introductory physics courses. My time teaching was formative, orienting me towards the efficacy of an interactive, experiential learning process where students develop and enhanced understanding of curricula via interactive modules. This experience in the classroom complements my eight years of experience as a one-on-one private physics tutor, which has provided me with insight into the learning processes of a diverse set of students, as I help them master the courses in question. \par

My educational experiences are buttressed by years of experimental biophysics research which gives me the applied knowledge base and technical expertise combined with broad philosophical study as an undergraduate which empowers me to answer questions with expertise and nuance. My works has given me a deeply grounded, functionalist approach to physics focused on how theory can be applied dynamically to better understand the world around us. As a result of these experiences and my undergraduate degree in philosophy, I have developed an academically broad, experimentalist perspective focused on how physics can be used as a tool to understand complex phenomena beyond the walls of the classroom.  For instance, advised by Dr. Richard Superfine, I studied the stochastic processes of nanoparticles in poroelastic media during my Master's thesis, using the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, a technique typically associated with financial modelling, to better understand strain-driven changes in nanoparticle-matrix interactions. My history of applying physics in surprising ways to understand emergent phenomena prepares me to show students that no matter thier interests, physics can serve as a tool for advancing thier understanding.\par

Thank you for your time, and I look forward to talking with you about this exciting opportunity in the coming weeks.\par

Sincerely, \par

\subsection{Masters Thesis}

\cvitem{}{\emph{Physiological Strains in Poroelastic Media Induce Asymmetric Transport.}}
\cvitem{Advisor}{Richard Superfine}
\cvitem{Thesis Comittee}{Dr. Richard Superfine, Dr. Greg M. Forrest, Dr. David Hill, Dr. Amy Oldenburg, Dr. Kerry Bloom }
\cvitem{Description}{Tracked quantum dots undergoing dynamic diffusion in poroelastic media using LSFM with unprecedented temporal resolution. Live imaging during physiological strain reveals that large solute transport is asymmetric during dynamic strains, with advection yeilding significantly more forward angular anisotropy during the relaxation stroke than the compression stroke. These results suggest single-particle effects could explain the macroscopic solute concentration enhancements reported by studies of poroelastic media under dynamic strain.}
